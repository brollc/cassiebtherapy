Couples Therapy

Are you there for me?

I love working with couples. In my experience, there is something uniquely powerful about the work that can happen when healing takes place within the relationship itself. Dr. Sue Johnson, the creator of Emotionally Focused Therapy, once said, “Relational wounding needs relational healing.” I have found this to be incredibly true. While the relationship between a client and therapist can offer an opportunity to experience relational healing, there is something especially meaningful about experiencing that with the person you share your life with. It is one thing to have a therapist hold space for a tender part of you. But when your partner holds a raw part of you tenderly? Magic.

I truly believe relationships have the potential to be the kind of healing, deeply connected love so many romance authors dream up. I also know, both from my experience as a therapist and simply from being human, that despite our best intentions, we often need help learning how to be that kind of partner to the people we love most.

Are you there for me?

To me, this question is at the heart of couples therapy. It is an incredibly vulnerable and important question to ask of our partners. And I don't mean it in the practical sense of, “Will you show up at the hospital if I'm in an accident?” Often, when I've heard this question, or some version of it, what we're really asking is something much more emotional.

  • Are you there for me?
  • Can I show you my emotions without you pulling away?
  • Will you stay if you see the parts of me I try so hard to hide?
  • Will you hold me when I'm in my deepest shame?
  • Can I still reach for you when I need comfort?

My goal as a couples therapist isn't simply to give you more information about how to be a better couple (although, yes, skills are part of it!). My goal is to help you have a different experience of one another.

In our sessions, we'll work to understand the attachment wounds and emotional experiences that influence how you show up in your relationship. We'll identify the patterns of communication and interaction that leave you feeling stuck or disconnected, and begin developing new ways of reaching for, responding to, and connecting with one another. Sometimes, that means building something entirely new. Other times, it means rediscovering a connection that has gotten buried beneath the demands and difficulties of life.

My hope for every couple I work with is that, over time, you develop a deeper understanding of one another and a greater sense of security in your relationship. That when life gets hard, you have a way of finding your way back to each other. And that if you were to ask your partner, “Are you there for me?” you could trust the answer.

So many different things can bring a couple to therapy. Over the 10 years I've been practicing, I've seen how much this has evolved. Couples therapy isn't just for healing betrayals and affairs or trying to repair a relationship in crisis. I regularly work with couples who simply want to strengthen their relationship, deepen their connection, and be more intentional about the life they're building together.

While I have experience working with many of the common reasons couples seek therapy, some of the couples I most often work with are those who:

  • Are experiencing significant relationship distress or feeling stuck in recurring patterns of conflict.
  • Are newly married and want to develop a stronger foundation, improve communication, and deepen their bond.
  • Are navigating parenthood, particularly the early years of raising young children.
  • Are navigating the demands of a high-pressure career (C-suites, executives, and high-pressure sales).
  • Want a deeper emotional connection with their partner but aren't quite sure how to get there.
